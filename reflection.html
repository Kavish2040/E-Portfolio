Critical Reflection

Critical Reflection

When I began this introductory composition course, I saw writing primarily as a tool for completing assignments and expressing personal ideas. I understood that different contexts required different approaches, but I had not yet grasped how deeply writing connects to community, culture, and power. Through analyzing discourse communities, studying rhetorical traditions, and exploring multimodal communication, I have come to see writing as a social practice that shapes and is shaped by the communities we participate in.

The discourse community assignment transformed how I think about belonging and communication. Writing about the Shivaji Park Veterans Cricket Club in Mumbai allowed me to see how specialized language, shared genres, and common goals create meaning within a group. More importantly, it showed me that joining any community requires learning its conventions while also contributing something new. This realization has made me more conscious of how I adapt my writing for different academic disciplines and professional contexts. I now recognize that writing is not just about correctness but about understanding the expectations and values of the communities I write for.

Analyzing Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's "The Danger of a Single Story" deepened my awareness of rhetoric's ethical dimensions. Adichie's talk demonstrated how storytelling can either reinforce stereotypes or challenge them, depending on who controls the narrative. This assignment pushed me to think critically about the stories I consume and the ones I tell. It also revealed that persuasion is not merely about winning arguments but about fostering empathy and understanding. As someone who has lived across cultures and speaks multiple languages, Adichie's message resonated personally. Her talk reminded me that my own experiences traveling to over twenty countries give me a responsibility to share diverse perspectives and resist single stories in my own writing.

The discussion posts allowed me to engage with complex theoretical concepts and apply them to real-world examples. Through Kress's work on multimodality, I learned that writing is just one mode among many for creating meaning. Swales's framework helped me understand how communities function through shared communication practices. The readings on rhetoric, from ancient Athens to modern university marketing, showed me that persuasion has always been central to human interaction, but it also raises questions about manipulation and power. These discussions taught me to read critically, questioning not just what texts say but how they say it and who benefits from particular rhetorical choices.

Perhaps the most significant shift in my thinking has been recognizing that writing is never neutral. Every choice I make as a writer, from the words I select to the genres I employ, reflects and reinforces particular values and power structures. This awareness has made me more intentional about my writing process. I now ask myself not just whether my writing is clear and correct, but whether it respects my audience, acknowledges complexity, and contributes something meaningful to the conversation.

Looking ahead, I plan to apply these lessons both in my academic work and in my role as CTO of a health tech startup. In technology, clear communication is essential, whether I am writing code documentation, explaining technical concepts to non-technical stakeholders, or persuading investors. Understanding rhetorical situations, adapting to different discourse communities, and using multiple modes effectively will help me bridge the gap between technical and non-technical audiences. Similarly, as I continue my studies at TCU and develop as both a writer and technologist, I hope to use writing not just to inform but to connect, persuade, and create positive change.

This course has taught me that strong writing requires more than grammatical correctness. It demands awareness of audience, purpose, and context. It asks us to think critically about power, representation, and ethics. Most importantly, it invites us to see writing as a way of participating in communities and shaping the conversations that matter. I am grateful for the tools this class has given me, and I look forward to continuing this journey of growth as a writer and communicator.
