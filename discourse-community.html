ENGL 10803.075 Introductory Composition

29 October 2025

The sun blazed down on the parched cricket field behind my apartment building in Mumbai and I watched as a bunch of old men, most of them in their sixties and seventies, played what looked like a casual game of cricket. It was May 2021, and I had completed my last high school exams, anxiously counting down the days until I would move to Texas Christian University in the fall of 2022. I considered the endless running left in the gap year and I started to feel restless, hungry for something that could give a structure for the remaining months before America. My grandfather, who I refer to as Dadu, had just moved in with us after my grandmother died the previous winter. For much of the day he sat quietly in his room reading newspapers and sometimes watching old Hindi films. The bright, quip-filled man who had taught me chess and stories of pre-independence India seemed to have folded into his own hollow core of bereavement.

One night when I was walking the neighbor's dog, I walked past the cricket pitch again and saw Dadu standing by a fence there watching the older men play. His fingers grasped the chain-link fence and something in his eyes, a longing or an echo of youth, drew my eyes to his face. I went over and asked him if he would like to come with them. He shook his head immediately. "I haven't touched a cricket bat for forty years, Kavish. These men would laugh at me." But I noticed how his eyes tracked the ball, the subtle shift of his shoulders when someone put a particularly good shot together. Well, the next day, I did something irrational. I walked out on the field during their evening game and asked if my grandfather could play with them.

The response surprised me. These were not random men playing casual cricket. They were part of the Shivaji Park Veterans Cricket Club, a group of retired professionals, former athletes, teachers and businessmen who had been playing together three days a week – Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday evenings – for the past 15 years. Their de facto leader, a big and tall man named Mr. Khanna, grinned and said: "Bring him next Thursday. We always welcome new members." That conversation precipitated me on an unexpected journey into a discourse community I had never expected to enter: that of a senior citizens' cricket club, for which I became not only a member, but also the bridge between my grandfather and renewed purpose.

When I had taken Dadu to the field on that Thursday, I planned just to leave him and go back home. But Mr. Khanna had different plans. "You play too, young man. We need a guy to let someone run for these old bones when we get tired." Minutes later, there I was with a cricket bat in my hand for the first time in years, standing among my fellow men who might have been my grandfather, uncles or retired teachers. To me, what began as a kind gesture toward Dadu became something profound for myself. I have spent the last 15 months from May 2021 to August 2022 and although I am home in Mumbai during my winter and summer breaks, this community has been at the center of my life. The Shivaji Park Veterans Cricket Club taught me about resilience, dignity, old-young friendship and the miracle of sport to heal and connect over an age gap.

The Shivaji Park Veterans Cricket Club is not just a discourse community; it fits with all six of John Swales's criteria for a discourse community. The club also has obvious common public objectives that are accepted by everybody and go well beyond playing cricket. The easiest common purpose is their mutual self-interest in maintaining physical health, and mobility via a structured ongoing exercise program. Average members are between sixty and eighty years old, an age when sedentary living can exacerbate the slide into decrepitude. Being part of a club that meets three times each week gives the program structure and accountability, which many appear to admit they would be without doing it on their own. Mr. Patel, a retired bank manager who joined after heart surgery, said his cardiologist had recommended regular exercise but acknowledged that "just walking seemed boring and it was easy to drop." Cricket provided a reason for him to keep turning up.

Beyond mere physical health, the collective community's ambition is of equal importance in fighting social isolation and loneliness which often accompany retirement and aging. For many, retirement was seen as a challenging transition from structured work lives to time that is unstructured, unlimited. The cricket club provides social structure, regular human interaction and a sense of belonging to something bigger. At one postmatch tea, Mr. Khanna put it eloquently: When you retire, society tells you that your productive life is over, but the club members don't feel spent. They have plenty of energy, information and stories to tell about their lives — and a need to be visible and recognized. This club affirms the fact that they do still matter, are still capable, are still competitive and most importantly, still very much alive in every meaningful way.

The club also adopts the shared objective of conserving cricket culture and heritage. In India, cricket is not a game alone — It's a cultural phenomena, A source of national pride and collective memories. Many of the club's members came of age playing cricket in the era before television was widespread, when neighborhoods would turn any open space into a makeshift field for matches. In between innings, members would swap stories about famous games they attended or their own glory days playing at the college level. When I arrived, they made sure to teach this computer science student who had spent more of his childhood playing video games than outdoor sports, about the intricacies and history of cricket in Indian culture.

I know that for myself, entering this community met some goal I never knew existed: meaningful connection with my grandfather and his generation. I'd grown apart from Dadu as I got more caught up with studies, tech and my pre-university plans overseas. He was of the pre-internet India world that seemed far removed from my own reality of smartphones and globalization. Because the cricket club gave us space to connect as equals, where his skills were valued and I'd see him as a whole person with strengths, friendships and dignity. The club placed a high value on this sort of interchangengenerational bridge-building; it was, in fact, an unstated objective.

The pathways of communication around the club are various and vital for its working. The primary means of being told there's cricket is for it to actually happen, which occurs at thrice-weekly sessions every Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday from 5:30 pm-7.30pm. But there is communication that goes beyond talking about cricket strategy. The pregame get-together, which starts around 5 p.m., is sort of a catchup session: Members give each other updates on their health, family matters, doctor visits and any struggles they may be having. After three straight missed sessions, the group began calling Mr. Deshmukh, and learned that he had fallen ill. Members of the group organized within a day to take turns visiting him, feeding him and ensuring that he did not feel lonely during his recovery.

Communication is electric onboards and customized to the age of the players in-match. Unlike the younger cricketers who would perhaps scream abuse, they cajole with gentle whispers and encouragements. When a catch is dropped or someone gets bowled out, teammates say encouraging things like "Good try, yaar" or "The sun was in your eyes." This is a mode of communication that reveals an unspoken awareness that at this stage in their lives and with these physical constraints, engagement and pleasure are more crucial than perfection.

Perhaps the most important method of information exchange are postmatch tea sessions. At the end of each session the group congregates for 45 minutes to an hour at a small tea stall outside the cricket ground. This custom is approached as reverently as the cricket match. On a cluster of plastic chairs arranged in a rough circle, sipping chai and sometimes swapping samosas, is where the deep communication of community gets discussed.

Topics range far and wide: current events, reminiscences about the past decades, health issues, family updates, philosophical reflections on aging and gentle teasing that betrays underlying affection.

Beyond in-person connections, the community upgraded to modern technology with a WhatsApp group that Mr. Khanna formed three years ago. The group of forty-seven also serves several other purposes: morning good-morning messages, often with an inspirational quote; a round-up of the weather's prognosis in advance of matches; health updates when members have medical appointments and need to fill each other in on treatment and prognosis; mobilisation about rescheduled training sessions. Its WhatsApp conversation has also broadened the feeling of community beyond space and time, as members have checked in on one another every day.

The club has many ways of doing its business. The game itself has more of a recipe, which over the years it adapted to keep in line with the ages and skill level of its members. Contrary to the formality of cricket, the club's games are more about flexibility and acclimatisation. Innings are shorter, rules have been adjusted for safety — if you've just had surgery it's a friend who runs for you while you bat. Scorekeeping constitutes another genre. Mr. Sharma has a physical scorebook in which he logs all the details from every match: the date, the teams, individual scores and some special moments. Not only did this scorebook - now in its fourth volume - track these young lives, it also has become an historical record of the community.

There is something uniquely modern about the WhatsApp good morning message. These messages adhere to familiar patterns, they usually consist of inspirational quotes in English and Hindi combined with images of flowers or sunrises. Messages like "Good morning friends! After all, age is merely a number. Keep your spirit young!" mirror the community's valuers around positivity, health and active aging. Another genre is the weather/schedule advisory which have standardized formats in the rohanda of Mr. Khanna or other senior members.

And then there is the oral teller in tea chats, perhaps the most culturally saturated genre. A cricket story might start by giving the time and place of an event, go on to convey a detailed account of a significant play (perhaps also showing how that play developed), proceed to establish that this was a peak moment in some larger narrative, then step back from describing the action in order meditate on what made it notable. Such tales entertain and educate new members about the history of cricket; credentialize the tale-teller; and construct a culture of public knowledge.

The clubs' technical jargon is even established as a tool for identifying who is on the inside and to aid communication. Cricket jargon is the foundation: "Stumps," "overs," and "wickets" are technical terms — as are "innings," or a team's turn in bat, and the "maiden over" it hopes to avoid. And beyond a basic vocabulary, the community has utilised more subtle phrases, such as "playing with soft hands," "finding the gap" or even perhaps "bowling a good line and length", that are indicative of a profound understanding of cricket.

The community's lexis also incorporates Hindi and Marathi words reflecting Mumbai's linguistic landscape. Terms of address like "ji" as a respectful suffix, "yaar" as friendship, and "arre" as an exclamation pepper conversations. Even members who speak fluent English naturally code-switch between English and Hindi. Community-specific expressions and inside jokes constitute another layer. Certain phrases carry meanings specific to this group's shared history, creating boundaries between members and outsiders while strengthening bonds among those who understand the references.

The threshold level of members demonstrates clear gradations of expertise. At the novice level are new members still learning community norms, cricket fundamentals, and social dynamics. When Dadu and I first joined, we occupied this position. Intermediate members have achieved basic competence and integration. They attend regularly, understand unwritten rules, participate fully in both cricket and social aspects, and have formed individual friendships. By my third month, I had reached this level.

Expert or senior members like Mr. Khanna represent the highest threshold. These individuals have been with the club for over a decade, serve as informal leaders, maintain institutional knowledge, and whose opinions carry particular weight. They organize schedules, mediate conflicts, make decisions about club matters, and serve as mentors. What distinguishes expert members most clearly is their role as culture carriers and community historians who consciously work to preserve the club's character.

After analyzing the Shivaji Park Veterans Cricket Club through Swales's framework, I can definitively conclude that it qualifies as a robust discourse community exhibiting all six characteristics with remarkable clarity. What makes this community particularly meaningful is how it addresses fundamental human needs that transcend sport. For the elderly members, the club provides structure, purpose, social connection, and dignity. For my grandfather, joining this community literally transformed his experience of grief and aging. The man who had retreated into isolation rediscovered joy, purpose, and friendship.

For me, the community offered unexpected gifts. I gained a window into aging that contradicted negative stereotypes. These men weren't pitiable or diminished. They were funny, wise, resilient, and fully alive. Most profoundly, the cricket club restored and deepened my relationship with Dadu. In that space, we weren't just grandfather and grandson separated by generational divides. We were teammates, fellow community members, and friends. When I left for TCU in August 2022, saying goodbye was nearly as difficult as leaving my family. Mr. Khanna made me promise to return every break. "You're one of us now," he said. "This is your community, always."
